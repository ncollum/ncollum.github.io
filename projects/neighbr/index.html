Neighbr: A Community Map for Windsor

school, team project, client project, angular, typescript

Neighbr is a working prototype of a community web app for Windsor, built by our team for a real client business as part of COMP 8967 at the University of Windsor. It brings everything happening in a neighbourhood onto one map: local events, community groups, businesses, posts, polls, and issues that residents report, such as a broken bench in a park.

The goal: give Windsor residents one place to see what's happening around them, report local problems, and follow those problems until they are fixed.

What the app does

  • A live map of the neighbourhood, with a different marker for each kind of item and filters like Now, Tonight and This weekend.
  • Local issues that residents can report and follow. Each issue moves through stages, and the map shows how many people are affected and when an issue is resolved.
  • Groups, events, businesses, posts and polls so neighbours can organize and talk about local decisions.
  • Acting for an organization: a user who runs a business or group can switch to acting on its behalf.

What I built

We worked in sprints from a Trello board, with each feature as a card. The features I built in Angular and TypeScript:

  • App layout and navigation: the main structure and menu every other page sits in.
  • Profile page and the "acting as" switch: lets a user switch between acting as themselves and as an organization they manage.
  • Issue stages and timeline: shows each step of an issue's journey, from reported to resolved.
  • Decline, needs more info, and permissions: the actions an organization can take on an issue, and the rules for who is allowed to take them.
  • Following posts and issues: so residents get updates on what they care about.
  • Business-only claiming: only businesses can be claimed by an owner.
  • Showing who acted for an organization: so it's clear which person took an action on behalf of a group or business.

Working with a real client

Every feature went through a GitHub pull request and was reviewed before merging. In our client demos, I present the issue journey: how a resident reports a problem, how it moves through each stage, and how neighbours follow it. Explaining features to a non-technical client taught me to start from what the user sees and wants, not from how the code works.

What I learned

Building for a real client is different from a class assignment. Requirements change after each demo, so small, well-scoped cards and clean pull requests made it much easier to adjust. I also learned how much permissions matter in a community app: deciding who can decline an issue, claim a business or act for a group is as important as the screens themselves.
